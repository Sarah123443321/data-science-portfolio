# Project 2:
# Predicting Undergraduate Student Stress

## Problem Definition
## Question: To what extent can self-reported academic, and lifestyle factors predict an undergraduate student's stress score?
My project aims to understand how both academic and societal stressors impact a student's anxiety rating. The goal is to utilize self reported metrics as a way to predict an individual's stress score. The target variable in my data set is stress score which is operationalized as a self reported number on a scale, a higher number coincides with more perceived psychological stress on an individual. This is classified as regression since stress score is a quantifiable continuous numerical value rather than a discrete category. Therefore the models I will use will be linear regression and a decision tree. These will aim to help show and predict an individual's stress score based on the self reported measures. The main groups who would benefit from exploring this topic would be University professionals both those focused on student well being such as advisors and counselors, but also professors and deans to help better accommodate students. The university professionals can utilize this data to help students become better learners, deploy proactive stress management techniques and make informed academic decisions. Similarly students themselves will benefit from understanding what causes their stress and how to mitigate these causes. This understanding of both students and educators could lead to better scores, more active participation and increased mental well being. This problem is increasingly beneficial as our day and age becomes more stressful. Students no longer need to just receive a degree. Pressure on students today to work, have internships, belong socially and manage an academic workload lead to high levels of stress. These have severe long term consequences such as academic burnout, drop out, lower retention rates, and long term health effects. Research shows that academic pressure and fears of failure are catalysts for student distress (Child Mind Institute, 2021). Furthermore, recent studies show that chronic low sleep levels (Gao et al., 2025) and individual financial struggles (Lara et al., 2022) drive stress levels higher. Modeling stress as an output of self regulated factors provides evidence as to which controllable habit has the most impact on wellbeing. This allows for targeted interventions. 

## Background and Context:
To understand this problem it is important to note student stress is not the result of one isolated factor. Rather, it is the combination of controllable and uncontrollable factors in one's life. When stress exceeds an individual's internal coping capabilities this can lead to detrimental outcomes. So being able to mitigate and control what causes one's stress is fundamental to wellbeing. Academic pressure is a leading cause to university students. GPA, coursework, deadlines, are driving factors in academic pressure Bedewy and Gabriel (2015). Environmental factors such as sleep level, social support, financial pressure all also increase a student's cognitive load. Strong social support is a number one factor that can help manage stress and increase a students well being Lara et al. (2022). Stress can start to be perceived by many students as burnout Olson et al. (2025). Many previous psychology studies have identified stress as a controllable and manageable emotion if targeted correctly. Stress is no longer an unpredictable emotion we can acknowledge and predict what will lead to high levels. 

## Data Description:
The data used in this project is sourced from the [University Student Stress Dataset](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/rc5htd5dfr/1) hosted on Mendeley Data.
My data comes from a study done in Bangladesh using 3000 undergraduate university students. The study reached out to almost 4000 students but had a total of 3000 responses. This is important to note as it does not encompass all students reached out to. Each row of the data set represents an individual student and his/her response. Each column is a different factor they reported on. The data is the results of a survey each student took anonymously. My target variable is stress score which is a computed variable for each student in my data set. The higher the score the more perceived stress. There are 18 variables available in my data set ranging from sex and age, to sleep hours, study hours, family support and screen time. In my models I will focus on my student based variables such as support, study hours, and screen time while leaving out age and gender. This is to avoid over fitting and demonstrate the variables that more likely cause student stress. Since the data was collected using students in Bangladesh this can lead to cultural barriers. However they are still university students so the data is useful. It is important to understand it may not be generalizable to our western society as different cultures put more influence on different things. Similarly since it was a survey all the data is self reported which can cause bias, this is helped slightly by the anonymity of the survey but bias may still exist. Similarly the survey captures a snapshot in time which may cause bias. If data is collected closer to exams students may be sleeping less and have more academic workload at the current time. So understanding timing is important. 

## Data Understanding, Exploration and Preparation:
The data has 0 missing values across all 18 attributes. The target variable stress scores show a bell shape showing a normal distribution with a mean of 11.92 and a median of 12. Assignment load and exam frequency both have high correlation to students' stress scores. With sleep values and family support having inverse relationships with stress. I selected the 8 features I feel were most important to a students well being and are important to understand. Selected these 8 features helps to lessen the chance of overfitting our model and truly target our research question. The heat map and summary statistics helped me select which variables were important to understand stress. We used isnull.sum and duplication.sum to check our data. Both these found zero which indicates no duplicates or missing data. Similarly outliers were kept as they demonstrate extreme values such as oversleeping, or no studying. Both these are important as these are real experiences students may have so taking them out would cause us to ignore real situations students experience. From 18 attributes we selected 8; these 8 were: study hours, class attendance, exam frequency, assignment load, sleep hours, screen time, peer pressure and family support. I chose to include the variables I feel directly influence a student's stress level. I chose to ignore variables such as age and gender as these have less of a direct impact and can not be manipulated by an individual. My continuous variables were standardized using a standard scaler which allows for direct comparison in a linear regression model even when units are different. To separate my data I used 80% for training and 20% for testing. I split the data before so that information from the test set was not used to fit or train the models, this helps prevent leakage. This means my training data did not influence my test data.

## Baseline and Model Development:
I coded for a training set mean baseline that predicts a stress score from the training data for each student rather than generalizing a score. So our mean is 11.92. This helps my models make better predictions rather than assume average stress for each student. I trained a linear regression model and a decision tree to interpret which factors contribute most. Linear regression was appropriate because stress is a continuous numerical variable I wanted to predict using its relationship to my selected features. The decision tree was appropriate to help determine which selected features could determine stress level the most. It shows the complex non linear relationships. I had an unconstrained decision tree and a tuned one to avoid overfitting our data. Both of my models I trained used 80 training 20 tests, with a fixed random state of 42 so it can be reproducible. I used five fold cross validation to test various combinations of my data. This made my decision tree better and increased the predictive performance to avoid substantial overfitting. To make my models fair I used my same 20% test group of students for all tests. I also avoided leakage by adjusting the standard scale only to my training data. 

## Model Evaluation and Selection:
I used mean absolute error, mean squared error and R squared to show how much stress variance of my model can be explained by the variables. I use these instead of precision and accuracy since its linear regression not classification. The baseline provides a benchmark by predicting the mean training stress score for every student. Its R squared of -0.016 shows that this simple approach does not predict individual stress scores. The Linear Regression model achieved an R squared of 0.703 and a much lower MAE, showing that the selected features provide more useful predictions than the average stress score. Furthermore our metrics demonstrate how the linear regression model wins on all aspects over both the tuned and non tuned decision trees. The tuned tree did score better than the untuned tree on all metrics. Linear regression was selected as the R squared was around 70 showing that 70% of stress variance can be explained. Similarly key factors such as screen time and workload followed smooth linear trends and strong correlation. The decision tree was important because it demonstrated which factors impacted stress scores the most, however the trade off is accuracy because it forces the linear trend to be concrete buckets. Linear regression is able to show the smooth trends and impact, however it does not capture non linear interactions. It also forces every increase to be linear, so an extra hour of screen time adds an automatic increase in stress. In reality the trends may be exponential where it trails off at a point.

## Model Interpretation and Limitations: 
Our model shows that our top influencing factors are screen time which add 3.23 points to the stress score for every one standard deviation of screen time, followed by exam frequency and assignment load. Top features that combat stress are family support and sleep hours which subtract the stress score. Study hours and peer pressure show very low influence. This shocks me because peer pressure feels like it would drive stress more. I was also surprised the most was screen time. This is not something I would have thought to research as a stress driver. My linear model accurately predicts stress for average scores but it buffers out for extreme scores. This could be due to factors not always being linear and buffering out at a point hours on screen may blend together and there is less difference between 9 and 10 hours as opposed to 4 and 5 hours. The coefficient tells us how much a variable impacts stress and the way it impacts (positive or negative relation). The residual shows the mistakes made between the true result and predicted result. Our model shows small balanced errors. We tend to under estimate extreme stressed students. Our models can lead us to conclude that stress can be predicted by specific variables in a student's life. This shows how controlling for these variables can help a student stress. Similarly, we can conclude the importance of family support and sleep. Schools should focus on distributing exams and work load while emphasizing the importance of maintaining family relations during this transitional period in a student's life where family communication may falter. We can not assume causation. We may want to assume that screen time causes stress however a stressed student may use screens as distraction thus making screen time high. We see correlation not causation. Similarly the model is not applicable to every individual. Some individuals may stray from the group predictions. It is important to understand every student is different and this is the average over a set of students. 

## Limitations Ethics and Reflections:
The first thing to note is the geographical location this data was collected from. Our data set is university students in Bangladesh. As noted before this means our data may not be generalizable to Western Society as academic structure, and cultural expectations influence societal norms. Similarly this data set does not cover all features that influence student stress. Financial pressure is a main feature left out. A student's job, loans and other money issues all can cause high stress. Similarly pre-existing mental health and known coping strategies all also influence stress on a student. Incorrect predictions from this data would impact students and faculty. Under predicting stress would impact at risk students who experience high stress to not be targeted and thus not get the proactive help they need. Over predicting stress is less of an issue but it could lead to the misallocation of limited resources. If I continued to use this data set I would control more for under predicting then for over predicting because it is important to target students experiencing high stress. This model could be appropriate for big group real world decision making but not as much on an individual level as each unique individual has their own factors that contribute to stress differently. But the model could be used at a university level to adjust exam schedules, promote family connection and reduce work load. It is important to understand that my model shows correlation not causation. These features may or may not cause one another but we can not automatically assume the cause. Furthermore it is important to note these are predictions not clear facts. One hour more of sleep does not automatically mean a percentage less of stress, it is rather a general trend in statistics. If I continued to explore this topic I would want to dive deeper into financial pressure as well as look at more western universities. I would like to compare how universities in the US compare to the Bangladesh data set. I also think it could be interesting to explore longitudinal data and track students over all four years of undergrad rather than one snap shot in time.
